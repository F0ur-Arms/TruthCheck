TruthCheck

Health claims travel in Hinglish. Verification shouldn't lose them in translation.

TruthCheck reads a claim the way it actually arrives — "protein kharab hai kidneys ke lie" — decomposes it, retrieves evidence from WHO, ICMR, PubMed and Indian fact-checkers, and returns a calibrated verdict with citations. Never a bare true/false. Never a fabricated source.

Most verifiers translate first, then forget what they lost.

Health misinformation in India rarely arrives as a clean English sentence. It's a WhatsApp forward, a voice note, a screenshot — colloquial, code-mixed, hedged. Translate-then-discard destroys negation and nuance before a single fact is checked.

What we don't do

  • Translate to English and discard the original
  • Ask an LLM for a confidence number and trust it
  • Look up one embedding match and call it evidence
  • Collapse "true for one group" into a universal verdict
  • Treat source popularity as evidence quality

What we do instead

  • Keep the original Hinglish/Hindi/English as ground truth
  • Compute confidence from measurable signals, calibrated
  • Retrieve support, contradiction, guideline & population evidence
  • Flag missing population/dosage qualifiers explicitly
  • Score evidence by tier × recency × specificity × population fit

Nine stages from utterance to cited verdict.

Deterministic code stays deterministic — retrieval, reranking, NLI and calibration are classifiers and formulas, not "ask the LLM." Only claim understanding, query planning and explanation get LLM reasoning.

01

Understand & route

Detect language, script and code-mix ratio. Route personal medical questions to a safety pathway — they never get a fact-check verdict.

language + script detection → routing gate
02

Decompose the claim

A compound claim like "whey protein is bad for kidneys and causes failure" becomes independently verifiable sub-claims with subject, population, outcome, direction and certainty.

structured schema, not a translated sentence
03

Generate adversarial queries

Every claim gets a support query, a contradiction query, a guideline query, and a population-specific query — never a single "does this exist" lookup.

support · contradiction · guideline · population · India-context
04

Hybrid retrieval

BM25 for exact terminology, dense embeddings for paraphrase and cross-lingual match, run per query lane against WHO, ICMR-NIN, PubMed and Indian fact-check corpora.

BM25 + BGE-M3 dense, reciprocal-rank fused
05

Rerank & score evidence quality

A cross-encoder reranks the pooled results against the canonical claim. Each passage then gets a computed score — source tier, recency, specificity, population match — not a fixed whitelist.

BGE-Reranker-v2-M3
06

Multilingual entailment

Each top passage is checked against the claim for support or contradiction — in the claim's own language, not an English-only model applied after lossy translation.

mDeBERTa-v3 multilingual NLI
07

Calibrate confidence, separately assess harm

Confidence comes from a trained calibration model over retrieval quality, entailment agreement and population match. Harm is scored independently — a low-stakes claim and a "stop taking insulin" claim never share a priority just because both are uncertain.

two axes, never one blended score
08

Route to human review when it matters

Low confidence, high harm, conflicting top-tier sources, or medication-cessation language — any of these pause the run for a human reviewer rather than guessing.

LangGraph interrupt + checkpoint
09

Explain, in the language it arrived in

The response mirrors the input's code-mixing pattern and cites only passages actually retrieved in this run — nothing the model wasn't shown can be cited.

closed-citation-list constraint

A taxonomy wide enough for a nuanced answer.

Never a bare true/false. "Insufficient evidence" is a successful output, not a failure mode.

SUPPORTED

Evidence directly and consistently backs the claim as stated.

SUPPORTED_WITH_CAVEATS

True mechanism, but the claim omits a population or dosage qualifier that changes the practical answer.

MIXED_EVIDENCE

Genuinely conflicting high-quality evidence, not just a missing qualifier.

MISLEADING

Technically not false, but framed in a way that produces a wrong practical belief.

FALSE

Directly contradicted by consistent, high-tier evidence.

INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE

Not enough high-quality evidence exists to determine this — stated plainly, not hidden.

NOT_VERIFIABLE

The claim isn't structured in a way that admits a factual check.

NOT_A_FACT_CHECK

A personal medical question, routed to the safety pathway instead of a verdict.

Specialist models do the arbitration. The LLM does language.

Fine-tuned compact models stay competitive with general LLMs on entailment and reranking, at a fraction of the cost and with far better calibration — so that's where they work.

About TruthCheck

What it is, what it isn't, and why it's built the way it's built.

A medical evidence verification engine for health claims in Hinglish, Hindi and English — not a chatbot with search bolted on. It decomposes a claim, retrieves evidence from tiered medical sources, checks entailment with a multilingual model, and returns a calibrated verdict with citations and a separate harm assessment.
Because a confidence number an LLM reports about itself isn't calibrated — a stated 90% doesn't mean 90% empirical accuracy. TruthCheck computes confidence from measurable signals (retrieval quality, entailment agreement, population match) through a trained calibration model, and uses specialist NLI/reranking models for the actual entailment judgment. The LLM's job is language understanding, claim decomposition and explanation — not final truth arbitration.
Translate-then-discard silently rewrites negation, hedging and sarcasm mid-sentence before any parsing happens — a "does X cause Y?" question can drift into an assertion. The original utterance is stored and reasoned about as the primary object throughout the pipeline. An English gloss exists only as a derived aid for retrieval queries and cross-lingual model input — it's regenerated, never edited in place, and never shown to the user as if it were the source claim.
Passages from WHO, ICMR-NIN, PubMed, government guidelines, and India-specific fact-check corpora (Vishvas News-style), each carrying a computed evidence-quality score — source tier × recency × specificity × population match — rather than a fixed whitelist or a popularity signal. Retrieval is hybrid (BM25 + dense) and adversarial: every claim gets a support query, a contradiction query, a guideline query, and a population-specific query.
Because it's a true and useful answer, not a fallback to hide. A system that fabricates confidence when the evidence is thin is more dangerous than one that says so plainly. INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE is a first-class verdict in the taxonomy, treated as a successful output.
No. A routing gate runs right after claim understanding and separates fact-checkable claims ("does protein damage kidneys?") from personal medical questions ("I have CKD, should I stop taking whey protein?"). The second class is routed to a safety pathway that never produces a diagnosis or personalized recommendation — it gives general, evidence-backed information with explicit guidance to consult a clinician, and is logged separately from fact-check volume.
This is the "missing population qualifier" pattern — e.g. "protein damages kidneys" is false for a healthy adult but conditionally true for someone with pre-existing kidney disease. When evidence splits cleanly by population, the verdict is SUPPORTED_WITH_CAVEATS, and the explanation states the population split explicitly rather than collapsing to one universal verdict.
No, by construction. The response generator receives a closed list of retrieved-passage IDs for that specific request and can only cite from that list — it structurally cannot introduce a statistic, citation or organization name that wasn't in the evidence set actually retrieved and scored in that run's trace.
They're two separate axes, never blended into one score. Confidence measures how sure the system is in its verdict. Harm measures what happens if the underlying claim is believed and acted on — medical actionability, severity, population vulnerability. "Eating mangoes at night is bad" and "stop taking your insulin, diabetes can be cured naturally" can have similar uncertainty but must never get the same priority, because one is inert trivia and the other can kill someone.
Yes — routed automatically whenever confidence is low, harm is high, top-tier sources disagree in direction, the claim is novel, or medication/treatment-cessation language is detected (a hard override regardless of computed confidence). This is a native LangGraph interrupt: the full state is persisted and a reviewer resumes the exact point in the graph rather than the whole pipeline re-running.
The architecture is designed for text, images (e.g. WhatsApp/Instagram screenshots — OCR first, VLM fallback only on low-confidence or layout-heavy input) and audio (Indic-tuned ASR with a language hint and per-segment confidence), all feeding the same claim-understanding stage once transcribed. This build's chat interface currently accepts text only; image and voice capture are on the roadmap.
