A social media post shows a man loudly claiming that he has so much confidence in his drink made with Eno, lemon, black salt and black pepper that it can even dissolve rocks when consumed. We fact checked and found the claim to be false.
A Facebook post claims that mixing lemon juice with Eno, black pepper (kali mirch), and black salt (kala namak) creates a powerful drink capable of helping with indigestion. The claimant even exaggerate further, saying that “even if someone eats rocks, this drink will digest them”.
Can this drink actually dissolve rocks if consumed?
No. There is absolutely no scientific basis for the idea that this drink can dissolve rocks in the human body. Rocks are made of hard minerals such as silica, calcium compounds, or other geological materials that require extremely strong acids or high temperatures to break down. The human digestive system simply does not function in that way. Even stomach acid, which is quite strong, cannot dissolve stones or rocks.
The stomach produces hydrochloric acid with a pH of around 1.5 to 3.5. This acid helps break down food, especially proteins, but it is not capable of dissolving solid mineral rocks. In fact, if someone were to swallow a rock, the body would not “digest” it at all. It would either pass through the digestive tract or potentially cause injury or blockage.
The ingredients in the viral drink do not change this reality. Lemon juice contains citric acid, but it is far weaker than stomach acid. Eno is mainly a mixture of sodium bicarbonate and citric acid that produces fizz when mixed with water. Black pepper and black salt mainly add flavour and may stimulate digestion slightly, but they do not possess any stone-dissolving properties.
So the idea that this mixture could digest rocks is simply impossible from a biological and chemical perspective.
Dietitian Shikha Sharma, MSc in Foods and Nutrition, Noida, explains, “The claim that a mixture of lemon juice, Eno, black pepper and black salt can dissolve rocks inside the human body has no scientific basis. The digestive system is designed to break down nutrients from food such as carbohydrates, proteins and fats, not solid mineral materials. Even the stomach’s own acid, hydrochloric acid, which is quite strong, cannot dissolve stones or rocks. Lemon juice contains citric acid, but it is significantly weaker than stomach acid, and Eno mainly creates a fizzing reaction due to the release of carbon dioxide gas when its ingredients react with water. This bubbling may look dramatic, but it does not mean the mixture has any ability to break down or dissolve hard substances inside the body.”
Does the fizzing reaction mean it is dissolving something inside the body?
No. The fizz you see when lemon juice and Eno are mixed is just a simple chemical reaction producing carbon dioxide gas. When sodium bicarbonate (in Eno) reacts with acid, bubbles form. This is the same reaction that happens when baking soda is mixed with vinegar in school science experiments. The bubbles may look dramatic, but they do not indicate that anything powerful is happening inside the body.
Once consumed, this reaction quickly settles in the stomach. The fizzing may temporarily relieve acidity by neutralising some stomach acid, which is why products like Eno are sometimes used for indigestion. But the effect is mild and short-lived.
Importantly, the bubbles have nothing to do with dissolving stones or breaking down hard materials. The reaction simply releases gas and forms salts and water. It does not produce any compound capable of dissolving rocks or mineral deposits.
Does this home remedy actually help with digestion?
Yes, but only to a limited extent. Some ingredients in this mixture may provide mild relief from temporary digestive discomfort.
Eno acts as an antacid. Its sodium bicarbonate content can neutralise excess stomach acid, which may help relieve symptoms such as acidity, bloating, or heartburn for a short period. Lemon juice may stimulate saliva and digestive secretions, which can sometimes make people feel that digestion is improving.
Black pepper contains a compound called piperine that can stimulate digestive enzymes and may support the breakdown of food. Similarly, black salt is traditionally used in small amounts in digestive preparations because it may help reduce gas and improve flavour.
However, this does not mean the drink “boosts digestion dramatically” or treats digestive disorders. Its effect is temporary and largely related to acidity relief rather than true digestive improvement. Regular digestive problems should always be evaluated by a healthcare professional rather than relying on home remedies alone.
Dr Durga Priyadarshini, PhD, Nutrition Researcher & Founder, Nutrition Research Hub, Chennai, says “Some ingredients in this mixture may provide mild relief from temporary digestive discomfort. Eno works as an antacid that can neutralise excess stomach acid for a short period, and black pepper contains compounds that may stimulate digestive enzymes. However, this effect is limited and should not be confused with the ability to dissolve stones or rocks. Viral claims often exaggerate simple digestive remedies and present them as powerful cures, which can mislead people. Good digestive health is supported through balanced eating, fibre intake, hydration and medical guidance when needed not through home remedies that promise unrealistic results.”
So this viral claim that lemon juice, Eno, black pepper, and black salt can dissolve rocks inside the body is scientifically impossible. The fizzing reaction in the drink may look impressive, but it does not translate into any stone-dissolving ability inside the human body.
At best, this mixture may offer temporary relief from acidity. At worst, exaggerated claims like these create false hope and spread confusion about how the body actually works.
When it comes to stones or digestive health, reliable medical advice, not viral remedies, remains the safest path.
