Poliovirus, an enterovirus, can potentially cause a debilitating condition referred to as poliomyelitis. Any one of the three closely related poliovirus strains—P1, P2, and P3—can induce the illness. The poliovirus spreads from person to person through oral contact with the saliva or faeces of an infected individual. Once viral replication has been established on the mucosal surfaces of the nasal part of the pharynx, the poliovirus can multiply in specialised cells in the intestines and enter the bloodstream to infiltrate the central nervous system, where it travels along nerve fibres. Once the virus spreads inside the nervous system, it can damage motor neurones, which are nerve cells that govern skeletal muscles.
The damaged nerve cells lead to acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) that is unable to repair, and the affected muscles exhibit a lack of nerve enervation. Patients with poliomyelitis typically experience more muscle weakness in their legs than in their arms. More severe paralysis of the abdominal, thoracic, and trunk muscles can result in quadriplegia. In the most severe cases (bulbar polio), the poliovirus targets the motor neurones in the brain stem, causing respiratory difficulties as well as difficulties swallowing and speaking. In the absence of respiratory support, bulbar polio can be lethal. While it can strike anyone at any age, children under the age of three account for nearly 50% of cases.
Due to the severity of the disease in May 1988, the World Health Assembly, which is the governing body of the World Health Organisation (WHO), decided to completely eradicate polio during its annual conference in Geneva. The primary objective of the global eradication initiative is to prevent the spread of the poliovirus globally and to implement strategies to impede its reintroduction. In light of the disease prevention of this dangerous illness, this article covers all the essential aspects of the polio vaccine.
What exactly is polio vaccine? What are its different types?
A polio vaccine protects against poliomyelitis, a highly contagious viral illness that primarily affects young children. Types of polio vaccines include the oral (painless formulation) and the injectable, inactivated form.
Oral Poliovirus vaccine (OPV): Several manufacturers worldwide develop trivalent OPV utilising World Health Organisation-provided Sabin vaccine seeds, with the exception of Pfizer’s polio Sabin type 3. The oral version of the POLIO SABIN vaccination contains live, attenuated polio viruses from the type 1 (LS-c, 2ab), type 2 (P712, Ch, 2ab), and type 3 (Leon 12ab) Sabin strains stabilised with magnesium chloride.
Furthermore, each dose of OPV contains traces of less than 25 µg of antibiotics, including neomycin and streptomycin. Preservatives and adjuvants are not added.
Vaccines that have been live-attenuated are very successful in preventing the transmission of infectious viruses and in protecting against disease. Nevertheless, developing live-attenuated vaccinations that are both safe and efficacious has proven to be a challenging process of trial and error. The OPV vaccine is an excellent example of a live-attenuated vaccine that works.
Inactivated injectable polio vaccine (IPV): Similar to OPV, the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) is composed of three poliovirus strains: Mahoney type 1, MEF-1 (Middle East Forces) type 2, and Saukett type 3. The IPV is given by an injectable route. The viruses for this preparation are cultured in human diploid (MRC-5) or Vero cells before being condensed, purified, and formaldehyde-inactivated.

Injectable polio vaccine contains all three virus serotypes (PV1, PV2, and PV3). It is given at the age of 6 and 14 weeks, with the OPV boosters. IPV is frequently given in conjunction with DPT, Hib, and Hepatitis B vaccines.
The oral polio vaccine, which comes in a bivalent formulation with serotypes 1 and 3, is administered orally as drops. Although they are accessible, monovalent (containing only one kind) OPVs are not frequently used. The government has reserved them to address the outbreak scenarios. Two drops of OPV are given straight into the mouth.
According to the Government of India’s UIP schedule, it is advised to take one dose of OPV shortly after delivery, three more doses at six, ten, and fourteen weeks of age, and a booster at sixteen to twenty four months. Additionally, it is utilised in supplementary vaccination initiatives, also known as Pulse Polio Days.
It is important to emphasise that children who have had IPV/OPV vaccinations should receive OPV on all “Pulse Polio” days until they are five years old. While IPV is the recommended vaccine, in case it is not easily accessible or available, the kid should receive three doses of OPV at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age. Moreover, in such cases, the child should be referred to a government facility for two fractional IPV shots at the six- and fourteen-week mark.
Do these vaccines for poliovirus pose a risk?
The two vaccines for polio are both well tolerated. However, there is a rare possibility that OPV might result in vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP), especially in newborns receiving the shot for the first time. Furthermore, this live attenuated vaccine may regain its virulence and transmissibility in low-vaccine populations, increasing the risk of vaccine-derived polioviruses (VDPVs), which have been linked to outbreaks. In addition, aseptic meningitis and encephalitis have occasionally been documented following OPV, especially in immunocompromised newborns.
Please be informed that these concerns are rare; OPV can be given in conjunction with other vaccinations, and there is no evidence that there is a decrease in immunogenicity or an increase in adverse event rates. Due to the common administration of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DPT) vaccine concurrently with OPV, side effects from the latter are frequently confused with OPV. The immune responses to OPV have not been altered by the concurrent administration of rotavirus vaccines. However, the antibody levels to rotavirus vaccines were generally lower when OPV and rotavirus vaccinations were given concurrently. This is particularly noticeable after the first dose of OPV.
Nevertheless, OPVs have certain benefits. The oral polio vaccinations are safe, effective, and provide individuals with long-lasting protection against the targeted serotype or serotypes. Furthermore, the OPVs are reasonably priced. The primary benefit of the oral vaccine is that, following administration, the vaccine virus multiplied in the gut and may have even spread to close contacts, causing passive immunisation. Most significantly, there is no requirement for medical staff, sterile needle syringes, or an elaborate cold chain infrastructure in order to administer oral polio vaccines.
Due to this, OPVs are simple to transport to difficult-to-reach locations and to give during widespread immunisation programs.
The inactivated poliovirus vaccine is thought to be reasonably safe. However, localised responses, such as injection site erythema, induration, and discomfort, are frequently observed with IPV. These are usually short-lived, mild responses. Most of the time, they go away on their own in two or three days and don’t need to be addressed anymore.
Certain polio vaccine formulations contain trace quantities of streptomycin, polymyxin B, and neomycin, which may cause allergic reactions in certain people. However, post-marketing surveillance has not revealed any evidence of such reactions. There have been no instances of transverse myelitis, thrombocytopenia, or anaphylaxis.
Who is not eligible for the poliovirus vaccine?
OPV should not be administered to children whose immune systems have been compromised by illnesses such as HIV or who are on medications like long-term high-dose steroids or immunosuppressive therapies, nor to people who have experienced a serious allergic reaction to an earlier dose of the vaccine, such as anaphylaxis, or who have a known, severe allergy to any aspect of the vaccine.
Indian Polio Vaccine Implementation Initiative: A Success Story
The polio vaccine in India has played a crucial role in the eradication of the viral infection. On October 2, 1994, India launched the Pulse Polio Immunisation Programme, at a time when India was responsible for almost 60% of all polio cases worldwide. After 20 years of waiting, India was officially declared “polio-free” by the World Health Organisation on March 27, 2014. The last case of polio was reported in Howrah, West Bengal, on January 13, 2011.
Eradication became possible when everyone, especially the most vulnerable and marginalised people residing in the most remote areas of the nation, had fair access to vaccinations. A strong commitment at all levels resulted in legislators, medical professionals, frontline staff, partners, and community volunteers cooperating to provide every kid with life-saving polio drops wherever they were—at home, in school, or while on travel.
Apart from ensuring universal vaccination coverage, the government endeavoured to tackle social and cultural concerns to eradicate vaccine apprehension in regions with inadequate healthcare infrastructure, as well as in marginalised and susceptible communities. This was accomplished by engaging the help of local leaders and communities.
On January 31, 2021, the inaugural Polio National Immunisation Day (NID) was observed as part of ongoing efforts to maintain the nation free of the disease. As a result, vaccinations were administered in around 700,000 booths on Polio Ravivar, also known as Polio Sunday, in 2021. These booths were staffed by about 1.2 million vaccinators and 180 000 supervisors. Volunteers and partners from Rotary, UNICEF, WHO, and other civil society groups supported these workers.
In order to preserve population immunity against wild poliovirus and to maintain its polio-free status, India continues to host one National Immunisation Day (NID) and two Sub-National Immunisation Days for polio each year. To provide even more protection, the Indian government has added the injectable inactivated polio vaccine to the routine immunisation schedule.
